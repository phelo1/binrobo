Verdict
Revision 5.1 applies Brian’s five answers honestly and mostly correctly. Its slope work reproduces exactly.
Two of its conclusions do not hold, though: “the hill is 8.5 % at most” and “the two contactors in series
mean one fault cannot keep the motors live”. It is still not ready to run unattended.
What holds:
  • The slope script. lane_grade.py on our sitl/data/route.json gives their table to the decimal.
  • The 12 % pitch was never a measurement. Nor was it Brian’s requirement. It came from us (see answer 2).
  • The stop table. Our hand model reproduces their wet-leaf stop table within about 15 %, except right at the
    holding limit.
  • The rev 4 timing bug is fixed. The trip clock now uses the measured trip time instead of c + 50 min.
  • Most carried rev 4 items are fixed on the drawings: a full-width nose, a 60 x 60 nose member, slip-resistant
    M10 corners, top blocks at +340 with the switch bodies first touched at +346, and a lowering guard referenced to
    the local ground.
What is wrong:
  • B-1 (carried, narrowed). 8.5 % is not an upper bound.
      – The 7.9 % comes from bilinear smoothing of a 25 m grid.
      – The raw EU-DEM cells along the lane step at 8.3 % (142-172 m) and 10.1 % (200-228 m). SRTM gives
         10 % over 50 m and 12 % over 25 m.
      – The empty robot, nose first on wet leaves (grip 0.22), holds only to 9.4-10.5 %. It stops inside the foam only
         to about 8.5-9 %.
      – The empty-robot problem therefore stays open until the hill is measured properly, and test 1 as written cannot
         resolve 8 against 8.5 %.
  • B-2 (new, cheap to fix). One shorted part defeats both contactors.
      – The braking-energy diode on sheet 15 bridges both contactors, so one shorted diode keeps the drive controller
         powered after any stop.
      – The all-in-series chain also contradicts the sheet’s own function table: lift-only and lowering-only inputs now
         drop the drive.
      – The B2-F relay (PNOZ X3: 3 NO + 1 NC + 1 semiconductor output) has too few outputs for the five loads
         drawn.
      – The B2-F contactors (KM-L8) have no auxiliary contacts for the drawn feedback loop.
  • The pause rule restarts with a person beside the robot. Only the front camera feeds it, so someone who
    walks past the nose drops out of view and the robot moves off 3 s later. A static false detection (a gatepost) holds
    the robot for 15 min and fails the trip.
  • The bye-law end time is 12:00 midnight in the legal text, not 21:00. The council’s summary page says 9.00
    pm, and that page is where our project’s “21:00” came from. With midnight, three pick-ups from 18:00 are legal.
  • The detector on the Radxa Zero 3W is plausible but tight. About 13 fps is realistic against 10 needed, and
    the camera’s other jobs also need it. Their fallback (a Raspberry Pi 5) has no NPU.
Scope: drawings, design, claims, and short hand checks. Siblings rev51-mech (MuJoCo) and rev51-sitl (ArduPilot) run
the simulations. ASSUMED marks a value with no data sheet behind it.

3. Do the KM-L8 contactors have auxiliary contacts? Not as listed.
   • KM-L8 is two generic SW100-class 100 A DC contactors with 24 V coils (Jazgot E1205, EUR 28.57 each). The

   • In the Albright SW80/SW100 family an auxiliary contact is an option: suffix A is a changeover microswitch rated 5

     mirror-contact contactors (+EUR 0-150, ASSUMED).

Status. This is the rev 4 B-1 with the slope uncertainty narrowed but not closed. It applies to B2-F too. It closes when
either of these is done:
   1. The hill is surveyed properly (see M-4, test 1) and is no steeper than about 8 % anywhere over a 1-2 m base. Then
      the 5.1 rule holds.
   2. The empty robot gets more braking or more weight on the drive wheels. Options are moving the battery and
      electronics aft, tail first (which needs rear foam of 100 mm or more and the rear lidar in the loop), or braked castors.

Until then, run empty trips nose first at 0.15 m/s only when no wet leaves are present, and treat leaf season as a no-go
for empty downhill runs. The robot cannot sense leaves, so this has to be a date or weather rule.

B-2. Stop chain on sheet 15: single faults and contradictions
   1. The diode bypasses both contactors.
        • Sheet 15 draws the braking-energy diode from the controller side of KM2 back to the battery side of KM1.
        • A diode’s common failure is a short. Once it shorts, the battery feeds the drive controller with both contactors
           open, and the e-stop, the soft nose and the tilt switch no longer remove drive power.
        • Nothing on 5.1 detects this: the auxiliary-contact feedback sees only the contactors.
        • Fix (cost about zero): fit one diode across each contactor (KM1 alone, KM2 alone), so a shorted diode
           bypasses only one contactor. Also keep the B2-F voltage sense on the controller side and check that the bus
           voltage falls below about 5 V within a few seconds of every stop.
        • The pre-charge path (K3 + 1 kOhm) across both contactors is harmless: at 24 V it passes 24 mA if K3 welds.
   2. Series wiring contradicts the function table. The left of the sheet puts all ten inputs in series into one
      two-channel loop, so any input drops both contactors. The table on the right gives some inputs different reactions:
        • top and bottom lift switches: “lift feed cut”;


                                                              4
       • under-bin ToF: “lowering blocked”;
       • “lift commanded while moving”: “lift feed stays cut”.
     As drawn, a ToF reading or a lift limit stops the whole robot and needs a manual reset. A relay with one loop
     cannot give different reactions. Fix: two chains, for example the PNOZ for drive plus e-stop, and a second relay (or
     the second B2-F relay pair) for the lift interlocks. Keep the ToF sensors as plain inputs to the lift driver’s enable
     through the lift relays. They are “not rated” anyway.
  3. Too few relay outputs. The PNOZ X3 has 3 NO safety contacts, 1 NC auxiliary and 1 semiconductor output.
     Sheet 15 needs KM1, KM2, two lift relays and pre-charge. Pre-charge must also close before the contactors, but all
     X3 outputs close together on reset, so this needs a sequence (companion drives K3, then reset). Draw it.
  4. The feedback loop needs auxiliary contacts the BOM parts lack (answer 3).
  5. Single-channel inputs. The watchdogs (“one signal each”) and the home-built speed and tilt electronics enter a
     two-channel loop through relays. This works if each drives a relay with force-guided contacts in both channels. Say
     so on the sheet.
  6. Brake supply not shown. “Spring brakes apply” needs the brake release to be powered only from the switched
     side. Draw where the brake coils are fed.
Realistic PL. With ten devices in series, ISO/TR 24119 fault masking limits diagnostic coverage to low. The parts are
used ones without MTTFd or B10d data, and the electronics are home-built.
  • After fixes 1-5, a fair statement is “category 3 structure, no PL claimed; engineering judgement about PL c for the
    e-stop path”. As drawn today, the diode makes the power path single-channel.
  • Their move from a PL column to “was aimed at” is the honest way to show this. For a private machine whose owner
    decided against certified parts, that is acceptable, provided the single faults above are removed and tested (M-4).


Major findings
M-1. Pause rule: what the front camera cannot see
Our numbers for the detection itself are in pause_calc.json and Fig 3: two hits in a row at 5 fps, 0.15 s processing,
misses assumed independent.

  • Geometry. With 5 m of pause range, the rule has margin down to about 40 % per picture. At the night rate, the
    robot stops about 3 m short of a standing person in 99.9 % of cases (5 m less 2.0 m), close to their 3.2 m.
  • Correlated misses. Real misses come in runs: dark clothes, a child behind the bin, rain on the lens. So the lidar
    stop (1.5 m) and the nose remain the actual protection. Rule 1 is a courtesy layer.
  • Simulation latency. In their “steps out 3.5 m ahead” runs the stand-out was found only at 2.2-2.6 m, which is
    2.3-3.3 s at 0.4 m/s. That is slower than the frame statistics predict (median 0.75 s), so the camera model has extra
    confirmation latency. Find out which latency is real before tuning X_PPL_PAUSE_R.
  • Restart beside a person (gap). pause_for_people resumes once the front camera has seen nobody within 5.5
    m for 3 s. A walker coming the other way passes the nose, leaves the +-55 deg view and walks along the flank. The
    robot moves off 3 s later with the person 0.5-1 m from its side and the bin.
       – Fix: resume only when the person has been seen leaving (tracked out of view going away), or once the rear
          camera or rear lidar confirms them behind the tail.
       – Until the rear sensors are in the loop, require 10 s or more clear plus a horn chirp before moving.
  • Pivot flanks. Rule 1 checks before a pivot with the front camera only. The flanks between x -0.4 and 1.0 m lie
    outside both camera cones (sheet 18, white areas). Rule 5’s “lidar check for anything else in the circle is still to be
    written”. A child beside the robot at the bin or the dock is exactly where pivots happen.
  • False pauses persist. A gatepost or wheelie bin seen as a person is detected again every frame, so the “clear for 3
    s” never comes. The robot waits 15 min, holds, and the trip fails. Test 8 would catch this, but the fix belongs in the
    design: require the camera person to match a lidar cluster that is not on the static map, or ignore a stationary
    detection that matches a mapped object after about 60 s.

Minor findings
  • m-1. Energy budget.
       – A trip uses 71-75 Wh. The runs used X_TRIP_WH=75 (from sitl_env_r5.sh, not 50), and the recommended
           80 is only 7 % above the measured trip.
       – Their own lane run at night with a heavier bin and less grip (P7) used 54 % more lane energy (25.5 against
          16.6 Wh).
       – Make the budget adaptive like the time estimate: the longest measured trip x 1.25.
       – The battery has room: an aged pack (80 %) at 0 C still ends three trips above the reserve, about 25 % left
          (our estimate).
  • m-2. Actuator shim note (sheet 17). “The actuator’s own stops fall at 331 and at -15 mm, whatever the built
    length” is possible only if the end-stop stroke is exactly 346 mm. If you shim for 331 at the top and the stroke is
    350, the bottom stop lands at -19, past the -18 block, so the actuator stalls on it. Shim to the bottom (-15); the top
    then falls at 331-335, which is 5-9 mm under the +340 block.
  • m-3. Stale text on rev 5 sheets. Sheet 13, loom L11 still reads “two contactor coils from the safety controller,
    the mirror contacts of both contactors”. Sheet 13, item 75 still says the rear lidar is “for coming down a steep pitch
    tail first”. Reissue sheet 13 with 5.1.
  • m-4. Hedge clearance. The least clearance in the mission runs was 0.197 m, against the planning rule of “never
    planned closer than 0.20 m”. Trivial, but the rule should then read 0.19 m, or the planner should hold 0.20 m.
  • m-5. Pivot nose speed. The peak dropped from 1.0-1.2 to 0.3-0.6 m/s, which is good. 0.6 m/s still exceeds the
    0.4 m/s straight-line rule, and the side faces’ foam depth is not given. At 0.6 m/s the robot moves 90 mm before
    the brakes even start (0.15 s). Cap the yaw rate so the nose corner (radius 1.34-1.39 m) stays at 0.3 m/s or less,
    that is 0.22 rad/s, and give the side foam depth.
  • m-6. Not re-run. The 60-mission score, the fault injections and the bend tests were not re-run under 5.1. The
    changes slow the robot and add stops, so a regression is unlikely. The pause rule, however, adds a new way to hold
    (M-1), so re-run the score once with false pauses injected.
  • m-7. Cost risks not in the range (Section 7).
       – The used LA36 at EUR 250-400 is plausible: used listings run EUR 250-450, for example Buy2Sell at EUR 399
           and EUR 449. The model code must match a 6.8 kN gear and a 350 mm stroke, otherwise expect new (EUR
           940-1,060, +EUR 540-810).
       – Contactors with auxiliary contacts: +EUR 0-150.
       – Companion upgrade: +EUR 60-130.
       – The frame, rails, nose and sensor rows are reasonable ASSUMED ranges.
       – Realistic all-in: EUR 4,100-5,100, and up to about EUR 5,800 if the actuator has to be bought new. Their
           figure is EUR 4,055-4,789. The arithmetic of their table is right (parts x 1.2034).

What revision 5.1 got right (confirmed)
  1. lane_grade.py on our route.json reproduces their table exactly (EU-DEM 13.7 m fall, 2.16 % mean, 7.9 % / 10 m
     at 145 m).
  2. The 12 % pitch, the step, the trench, the pothole and the leaf zones were assumptions, not measurements. They say
     so plainly, which is correct.
  3. Their stop table matches our hand model within about 15 % away from the holding limit (Fig 2).
  4. The weak case is correctly identified: empty, nose first, wet leaves, more than half the weight on the castors.
  5. Tail-first driving is rightly dropped while the rear has only 25 mm of foam.
  6. The trip clock uses the measured trip time (the rev 4 c + 50 bug), under X_BYELAW=1.
  7. Mission timings are as stated in their result files: three drops from 18:00 back at 21:31, three pick-ups from 16:30
     back at 20:16, 0.41-0.42 of charge left.
  8. The pause rule is coded as described and logs its pauses. The 2-minute message and the 15-minute hold are sensible.
  9. Their limits paragraph is honest: the camera rates are assumed, there is no rear camera in the simulation, and false
     pauses are not modelled.
 10. Two contactors in series are kept despite “no certified parts”. That is the right call, once B-2 is fixed.
 11. The cost arithmetic is consistent, and used LA36 prices are in their range.
 12. The carried mechanical items are fixed: corners, stops, top tie, nose member, nose width.
